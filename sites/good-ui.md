# Good UI

## What it is

GoodUI is run by Jakub Linowski, a Toronto-based experiment designer. It collects results from A/B tests that companies and practitioners share, and groups them into repeatable UI patterns. The patterns page counts 141 patterns from 642 tests: 168 winners, 43 losers and the rest not statistically significant. According to the pricing page, the tests cover about 149 million visitors. There is also a "Leaks" section on experiments spotted on sites such as Etsy, Amazon and Booking.com (112 so far), plus the site's original list of 75 UI ideas written before the testing began.

## When to open it

- When you are choosing what to test on a landing page, product page, signup or checkout and want to start with ideas that have a track record.
- When someone suggests a change (sticky CTA, countdown timer, fewer form fields) and you want to know whether it has won or lost elsewhere.
- When you want a free list of conversion ideas to start a design review.

## Most useful

- **Browse by screen, metric and tag**: home and landing, product, listing, checkout, pricing, cart, signup, thank-you; leads, signups or sales; forms, headlines, pricing, popups, buttons, social proof.
- **Pattern pages**: each shows how many times the pattern was tested and on which screens and devices, with A/B screenshots and a short note on each test.
- **Statistical power is shown for every test**, and the homepage can sort tests to only those with 80% power at a 5% minimum detectable effect. That makes it easy to skip weak tests.
- **Losing tests are published too**, so you can see which popular ideas tend to fail.
- **The free 75 ideas**: short "try X instead of Y" pairs such as a one-column layout, repeating the CTA on long pages, or social proof instead of talking about yourself.

## Using it with agents

There is no llms.txt (404), API, MCP or export. Pages are server-rendered, so an agent can read the free pattern names and descriptions, but the win rates show as "X.X%" unless you are signed in. Don't paste members-only numbers into prompts or shared files, because the licence is per user. A safe approach is to name the patterns in your brief ("use pattern #41, sticky call to action, on mobile") and let the agent build them.

## Watch out for

- The headline numbers only appear after you pay. For free readers, a pattern page shows what was tested but not whether it helped.
- A win on one site is not a promise for yours. The site's own FAQ says the results are about probability, not guarantees.
- Most tests come from ecommerce, lead generation and SaaS funnels, so the patterns lean heavily toward conversion.
- The homepage leads with a newsletter form and a strong sales pitch. You can ignore both and still browse.

## Reusable ideas

- Show the sample size and statistical power next to every claim that a design "works".
- Keep losing and flat results next to winners so a pattern's track record is honest.
- Give every pattern a stable number and name so teams can refer to it in briefs and tickets.
- Write design ideas as "try X instead of Y" so each one is a testable swap.
